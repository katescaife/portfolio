Teaching judgment: a two-week virtual workshop for security solutions architects

Global audience · live-virtual · multi-session · 4.5/5 across 143 responses

The problem

SA leadership came to us with two connected concerns: solutions architects were leaning on pre-built Navattic click-through demos instead of building custom demos mapped to each customer's situation, and proof-of-value (POV) win rates needed to improve. The gap wasn't knowledge — these are deeply technical people who know the product. The gap was judgment: reading a specific customer, deciding what to show, scoping what to prove.

The design principle

That diagnosis drove everything. You don't fix a judgment gap with a lecture on how to request a POV — smart people find that condescending, and they tune out. So the design rule for the whole program was judgment over steps: every session had to put learners inside a realistic decision and make them commit to a position before hearing anyone else's.

One early test of that principle: a facilitator wanted to deliver his well-worn talk on discovery and "finding the pain." He's genuinely passionate about it — but everyone in the audience had seen it, and it wasn't what leadership asked us to fix. Rather than cut him, I redesigned the session around his expertise: keep the pain framework, but use it as the mapping layer between discovery notes and a custom demo talk track. His passion stayed; the lecture became a working session.

How it worked: one customer, carried all the way through

I built a fictional company profile and a deliberately sloppy handoff note — an SE's raw discovery notes passed to the SA, with hidden gems buried in the mess for anyone paying attention. That artifact became the spine of the program:

  • Demo session. Learners arrived with a draft demo script based on the handoff note. In breakout teams, they compared approaches and designed a demo script together — including the discovery questions they'd ask during the demo and the aha moments they'd engineer.
  • POV session. The facilitators originally planned to present how to scope and request a POV. Instead, we continued the story: the customer loved the demo, and now wants a POV — with 11 use cases and a four-month timeline. Teams had to weigh which use cases would actually change minds within a realistic window, defend their picks to the larger group, and openly debate what a reasonable timeline looks like. Mid-exercise, we introduced a surprise scope-creep moment and made them choose a response.
  • Capstone lab. The program ended in a four-hour hands-on lab (built by a strategist colleague; I designed the experience around it). To recreate the energy of an in-person event in a virtual format, we ran multiple collaborative Webex spaces simulating the "ask your neighbor" dynamic — plus one designated quiet room for people who work best alone.

I also designed and built an interactive coverage-vs-noise scoring game for the detection engineering session — an HTML exercise where learners curated detections for a fictional customer and competed for the best balance of coverage against alert fatigue. It was cut for time in the first delivery when the facilitator's content ran long, which taught me something about program design too (see below) — but the concept generated real excitement, and it validated a format I've since rebuilt. A fictionalized version — new customer, new detection set, and a revised scoring model that fixes a flaw I found in the original — is playable on this site.

Constraints

This was designed as a live in-person event; budget cuts moved it to live-virtual late in planning, and people weren't happy about the change. Much of the experience design above — the persistent fictional customer, the breakout structures, the neighbor rooms — was about earning back the engagement that in-person would have given us for free.

Results

The post-survey came back at 4.5 out of 5 across 143 responses. Qualitative feedback consistently called out the realism: "this was probably the best workshop I've participated in — and my teammates said the same," "very realistic and well put together," and "a good, thought-provoking exercise."

"Having this framework to design demos that address customer pain points is great." — the feedback I value most, because it means the judgment structure landed, not just the sessions.

What I'd do differently

Two things, both mine to own even where I didn't make the original call:

  • Time zones. Some non-Americas sessions landed at genuinely unfair hours — one region was asked to log in around 7 a.m. I didn't set the schedule, but I should have caught it and pushed back. Global programs deserve global scheduling from day one, not as an afterthought.
  • Rehearse the tools, not just the content. Our facilitators were seasoned presenters but new to some of our interactive tooling, and in the first regional run, that showed. A proper train-the-trainer session focused on mechanics, with a full dry run, would have cost a few hours and bought a much smoother first delivery. That's now part of my program template.
