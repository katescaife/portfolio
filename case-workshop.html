Teaching judgment: a two-week virtual workshop for security solutions architects

Global audience · live-virtual · multi-session · 4.5/5 across 143 responses

The problem

SA leadership came to us with two connected concerns: some solutions architects were leaning on pre-built demos instead of building custom demos mapped to each customer's situation, and proof-of-value (POV) win rates needed to improve. The gap wasn't knowledge. These are deeply technical people who know the product. The gap was judgment: reading a specific customer, deciding what to show, scoping what to prove.

The design principle

That diagnosis drove everything. You don't fix a judgment gap with a lecture on how to request a POV. Smart people find that condescending, and they tune out. So the design rule for the whole program was judgment over steps: every session had to put learners inside a realistic decision and make them commit to a position before hearing anyone else's.

One early test of that principle: a facilitator wanted to deliver his well-worn talk on discovery and "finding the pain." He's genuinely passionate about it, but everyone in the audience had seen it, and it wasn't what leadership asked us to fix. Rather than cutting it, I redesigned the session around his expertise: keep the pain framework, but use it as the mapping layer between discovery notes and a custom demo talk track. His passion stayed; the lecture became a working session.

How it worked: one customer, carried all the way through

I built a fictional company profile and a deliberately sloppy handoff note: an SE's raw discovery notes passed to the SA, with hidden gems buried in the mess for anyone paying attention. That artifact became the spine of the program:

  • Demo session. Learners arrived with a draft demo script based on the handoff note. In breakout teams, they compared approaches and designed a demo script together, including the discovery questions they'd ask during the demo and the aha moments they'd engineer.
  • POV session. The facilitators originally planned to present how to scope and request a POV. Instead, we continued the story: the customer loved the demo, and now wants a POV, with 11 use cases and a four-month timeline. Teams had to weigh which use cases would actually change minds within a realistic window, defend their picks to the larger group, and openly debate what a reasonable timeline looks like. Mid-exercise, we introduced a surprise scope-creep moment and made them choose a response.
  • Product-track deep dives. The program then branched into two product-focused tracks that carried the same thread. One walked through detection curation for this customer: which data sources actually give detections good signal (whether firewall data earns its place was a lively debate) and how to balance coverage against noise. The other moved into security automation, scoping a POV for that product: teams used the same customer profile to pick the automations worth proving inside a POV window, weighed custom playbooks against pre-built ones, whiteboarded the builds, and defended their choices to the larger group. Each track teed up its own hands-on lab.
  • Capstone lab. The program ended in a four-hour hands-on lab (built by a strategist colleague; I designed the experience around it). To recreate the energy of an in-person event in a virtual format, we ran multiple collaborative Webex breakout rooms that attendees could opt into rather than being dropped into, simulating the "ask your neighbor" dynamic, plus a designated quiet room for people who work best alone.

The detection track’s coverage-versus-noise discussion also sparked an interactive scoring game I designed and built: learners curate detections for a fictional customer and compete for the best balance of coverage against alert fatigue. A scaled-down recreation, rebuilt for this portfolio with a new fictional customer, is playable here.

Results

The post-survey came back at 4.5 out of 5 across 143 responses. Qualitative feedback consistently called out the realism: "this was probably the best workshop I've participated in and my teammates said the same," "very realistic and well put together," and "a good, thought-provoking exercise."

"Having this framework to design demos that address customer pain points is great." That's the feedback I value most: it means the judgment structure landed, not just the sessions.

What I'd do differently

Two things, both mine to own even where I didn't make the original call:

  • Time zones. Some sessions landed at unfair hours for parts of the audience: one country started around 7 a.m. while the rest of its region logged in at 9. Starting a couple of hours later would have been less comfortable for those of us supporting from the Americas, but better for the attendees. That's the right trade, and I should have pushed for it. Global programs deserve global scheduling from day one, not as an afterthought.
  • Dry-run the tools, not just the talk track. We did run a full dry run, but we never fired the interactive tooling live during it. That made the first regional delivery the first time facilitators saw the polls and breakout mechanics in motion, and it showed. Running those live in rehearsal would have cost nothing; it was an oversight, not an effort problem. Live-firing every interactive element is now part of my rehearsal template.
