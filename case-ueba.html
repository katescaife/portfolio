Case study · Content transformation

From encyclopedia to learning experience: converting a 25-page SME brain dump

11-module micro-learning series · 3 role-based journeys · 5-star rating · 500+ completions

The ask

A subject-matter expert wrote down everything a solutions architect would need to know about a behavioral analytics security product (UEBA) — 25 pages of it. The content was technically excellent and genuinely comprehensive. Leadership needed learning built from it for the field's enablement plan, serving three audiences: sales, sales engineers, and solutions architects.

The diagnosis

The document wasn't wrong anywhere — it just wasn't a learning experience. Three specific problems:

  • It was organized like an encyclopedia. Encyclopedias are great when you already know what you're looking for. Learners, by definition, don't. The structure served retrieval, not acquisition.
  • The discovery section was a flat list of sample questions. No logic connecting symptom to consequence — a rep could recite the questions without understanding why any answer mattered or where it should lead next.
  • The technical depth had no "so what." An entity risk score update was documented as a formula, full stop. Accurate, and useless to someone who needs to explain to a customer why it matters to them.

The design decisions

  • Split prerequisite from reference — don't make one artifact do two jobs. The original doc, lightly revised, stayed alive as the reference material — the thing you search when you already know what you need. The learning became a separate micro-learning series of 11 modules — the prerequisite that builds the mental model first. Killing the doc would have destroyed real value; the fix was giving each format the job it's good at.
  • Route by role, not one path for everyone. An HTML front door recommended and linked specific modules for each audience — sales, SEs, and SAs each got a tailored journey through the same series instead of a single fire-hose sequence.
  • Give discovery a logic, not a list. The flat question list became an iceberg: the customer's visible symptom at the surface, the deeper operational and business consequences beneath it. The imagery did real instructional work — a rep who understands the iceberg can generate their own questions instead of reciting ours.
  • Turn the demo link into a discipline. The original pointed to a Navattic click-through and stopped. The module rebuilt demoing as show → explain → validate: show the capability, explain what the customer is seeing and why it matters to them, validate against the customer's own environment and pain.
  • Put troubleshooting where the trouble happens. Instead of a troubleshooting section at the back, field-relevant warnings were interwoven as "here's what to know and watch for" callouts inside the module where each issue actually arises — knowledge in the flow of the concept, not appended to it.
  • Diagram the architecture as a story. Simple, clean diagrams walked component pipelines from what it is → why it matters → how you use it — the same arc every module followed.

An honest note on tooling

The series was built in Articulate Rise — nothing technically groundbreaking, and that's rather the point. The transformation was information architecture and pedagogy: deciding what the doc should stop trying to do, sequencing for a mental model, and connecting every technical fact to a field conversation. The tool was incidental.

Outcome

The series currently holds a 5-star learner rating with over 500 completions, shipped as designed: micro-learning as the prerequisite, the revised original doc as the reference.

The quieter outcome mattered just as much: this was my first project with this SME, and the transformation made her a full convert to what instructional design brings. We've partnered on several projects since — expertise flowing in, learning experiences flowing out, trust compounding.
