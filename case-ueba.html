Case study · Content transformation

From encyclopedia to learning experience: converting a 25-page SME brain dump

11-module micro-learning series · 3 role-based journeys · 5-star rating · 500+ completions

The ask

A subject-matter expert wrote down everything a solutions architect would need to know about a behavioral analytics security product (UEBA): 25 pages of it. The content was technically excellent, but the plan of record was simply to take it as-is, break it up into modules by topic, and drop it into the field's quarterly enablement plan as required reading. Another designer had already made a first attempt, dropping the text into paragraph blocks and accordions to chunk it, and when the security portfolio moved to me, the project came with it.

As soon as I looked at it, I knew simple chunking without any reframing or supportive learning elements wasn't the answer. Breaking dense text into smaller pieces of text isn't learning design. A knowledge dump makes perfect sense to the expert who wrote it; learners aren't experts yet, and they won't absorb it the way she would. That's where we come in: translating SMEs so people can actually learn the material without drowning in it. Sometimes it's as much about what doesn't go into the training as what does. The uncomfortable part: the SME had already reviewed much of the earlier work, so rebuilding meant asking her to review everything again. I rebuilt one module and sent it over to see how it landed. She loved it, and I carried on through the rest.

The diagnosis

The document wasn't wrong anywhere. It just wasn't a learning experience. Problems like these ran all through the document; here are three examples:

  • It was organized like an encyclopedia. Encyclopedias are great when you already know what you're looking for. Learners, by definition, don't. The structure served retrieval, not acquisition.
  • The discovery section was a flat list of sample questions. No logic connecting symptom to consequence, so a rep could recite the questions without understanding why any answer mattered or where it should lead next.
  • The technical depth had no "so what." For example, an entity risk score update was documented as a formula, full stop. Accurate, and useless to someone who needs to explain to a customer why it matters to them.

Discovery and demo ended up needing the most net-new material, but nearly every section had its own version of these issues.

The design decisions

  • Split prerequisite from reference: don't make one artifact do two jobs. The original doc, lightly revised, stayed alive as the reference material, the thing you search when you already know what you need. The learning became a separate micro-learning series of 11 modules, the prerequisite that builds the mental model first. Killing the doc would have destroyed real value; the fix was giving each format the job it's good at.
  • Route by role, not one path for everyone. An HTML front door recommended and linked specific modules for each audience: sales, SEs, and SAs each got a tailored journey through the same series instead of a single fire-hose sequence.
  • Give discovery a logic, not a list. The rebuilt module anchored discovery in the product’s world: the real pains a customer can resolve with behavioral analytics, where they sit on the SOC maturity curve, and what qualifies a good fit. An iceberg visual carried the finding-the-pain idea, with the visible symptom at the surface and the operational and business consequences underneath, so a rep could reason toward their own questions instead of reciting ours.
  • Turn the demo link into a discipline. The original pointed to a Navattic click-through and stopped. The rebuilt module anchored on a gold-standard demo, with callouts on exactly what made it strong, and taught demoing as show → explain → validate: show the capability, explain what the customer is seeing and why it matters to them, validate against the customer’s own environment and pain.
  • Make POV scoping a reflex, not a checklist. The POV section was decent, but it was another judgment-over-steps moment. I made sure qualifying criteria and scoping resources were in easy reach, then added a red-light, yellow-light, green-light flashcard drill: quotes from fictional customers, things they might actually say before or during a POV, and the learner calls it. No-go, remediate, or proceed.
  • Put troubleshooting where the trouble happens. Instead of a troubleshooting section at the back, field-relevant warnings were interwoven as "here's what to know and watch for" callouts inside the module where each issue actually arises, knowledge in the flow of the concept, not appended to it.
  • Diagram the architecture as a story. The original either described data flows in text or bullets or showed them as screenshots of sprawling working diagrams, every source and action visible at once. Accurate, but far too much too early. We built clean, simple diagrams that walked each pipeline from what it is to why it matters to how you use it, building the mental model before revealing the full web of sources and actions.

An honest note on tooling

The series was built in Articulate Rise. Nothing technically groundbreaking, and honestly, that's the point. The transformation was information architecture and sequencing: deciding what the doc should stop trying to do, supporting the building of mental models, and connecting every technical fact to a field conversation. The tool was incidental.

Outcome

The series currently holds a 5-star learner rating with over 500 completions, shipped as designed: micro-learning as the prerequisite, the revised original doc as the reference.

The quieter outcome mattered just as much: this was my first project with this SME, and the transformation made her a full convert to what instructional design brings. We've partnered on several projects since, and she's now one of my strongest SME relationships.
